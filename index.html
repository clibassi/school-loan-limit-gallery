School Loan Limit Gallery

A set of examples of US colleges and programs that set their own lower federal loan limit, or that lend their own money to students. Each example shows the school's page with the key sentence highlighted, a plain explanation, and a link to the source. These are examples only; the gallery makes no claim about how common any of this is.

How to read the tags
  • The first two sections separate schools that set a lower limit on their own from schools that say they are doing it because of the court case over which programs count as professional.
  • Gives its own reason: the school's page explains the limit in terms of borrower protection, debt, cost or similar, without mentioning the court case.
  • Gives no reason: the page states a lower figure and says nothing about why.
  • Reserves the right and Lower amount by default: the school's policy allows a lower limit, or it offers less unless the borrower asks for more.
  • Offers the full federal amount and Private lender, no school money are shown for contrast. They are not school-set limits or school lending.

What the examples show

  1. Four schools set a lower limit without citing the court case. The University of Tennessee Health Science Center gives borrower-protection reasons for limiting seven health programs, although all seven are programs the court case covers.
  2. The other three are chiropractic colleges that list less than the federal $50,000 a year for new students: Palmer and Northwestern Health Sciences at $20,000 per term, and National University of Health Sciences at $40,000 a year. Each page describes the changes as federal. Only Palmer calls the figure an institutional limit, and a post on its site gives the arithmetic: $20,000 across ten trimesters reaches the $200,000 total at graduation.
  3. Other chiropractic colleges list the full $50,000, including Cleveland University-Kansas City, whose program has the same structure as Palmer's. So the lower figure is a choice, or a misreading, by some schools and not a federal rule.
  4. Eighteen schools say they are limiting loans because of the court case over which programs count as professional. They hold the affected programs at the graduate limit, usually stated as $20,500 a year.
  5. Two more, Michigan State University and the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center, list $20,500 for such programs on pages dated after the court order, without saying it is a choice.
  6. School lending is rare. The law schools at the University of Kansas and Washington University in St. Louis are the only two found lending their own money in response to the federal limits. Midwestern University's loan predates the law, and three more schools have only announced plans.
  7. The nine schools that stopped offering federal loans, or one type of federal loan, give their own reasons, mostly student debt or low use. None ties the decision to the 2026 limits, and some decisions predate them.
